Use the guide to apply the storytelling techniques with our own lead, environments, plots, compositions, and voice.

## Build the scene before choosing the angle

Start with a simple overhead map showing the lead, the important objects, the direction of travel, and where each camera sits. Label one or two landmarks that survive the cut. In the reference, road markings, lamps, and the crossing pole orient us from across-road, side, high, and rear views. Our scene can use its own fixed landmarks, such as a handrail, numbered doors, or a cracked tile.

| Camera mode | Use when | Continuity check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **First-person POV** | The lead notices, reaches for, inspects, or searches for something. Show a hand, sleeve, eyeline, or head turn that makes the ownership of the view clear. | Keep the camera at the expected eye height and on the right side of the landmark. Match the reaching hand, sleeve, grip, and direction with the third-person shot. |
| **Third-person wide or medium** | The viewer needs to see the body, balance, route, other figure, or change in distance. Useful just before and after a subjective insert. | Keep the character's pose, support, and screen direction consistent. If either character crosses the axis, use a visible move or a readable wide to establish the new relationship. |
| **Rear or over-the-shoulder** | The viewer needs to look down the lead's sightline while still seeing the lead and the far subject in the same picture. | Leave enough foreground shoulder or head to establish who is watching. Keep the target readable in depth. |
| **Reaction close-up** | A discovery changes what the lead thinks or does. It can hold through a pause. | Give the audience one readable change in gaze, jaw, grip, or posture. Cut close enough to see it on a phone. |
| **Detail insert** | A prop or physical clue matters right now. Show the connection between cause and result. | Establish its position in a wider shot, match contact frames, then show what changed when returning to the main action. |
| **Overhead or wide reset** | A reversal, move, or location change makes the geometry harder to follow. | Let the viewer locate the lead, destination, and obstacle before the next tight angle. |
| **Memory, possible outcome, or internal cutaway** | An explanation adds hidden information that the present scene cannot show. | Give the cutaway its own clear treatment. Resume the present scene on the same action, posture, and prop state. |

A practical sequence is **place → notice → proof → reaction → new action**. Use only the views that the moment needs. As an example, a wide shows our lead beside a door, their POV finds a mark on the handle, a close-up shows their eyes move toward the next door, and a side view shows them try it. The same environment produces four meaningful images by changing the job of the camera.

## Zooming, looking-around POV, and intensifying motion

Apply these camera and motion-intensification techniques from the [Nightmare Roulette study](source-study.md#zooming-looking-around-pov-and-intensifying-motion) to original scenes using our lead's [cinematic anatomy angles](../../assets/series-lead/angles-sheet.png), [face tilts and gazes](../../assets/series-lead/face-angles.png), [animated face & camera reference reel](../../assets/series-lead/anatomy-prototype/cinematic-face-camera-reel.mp4), and [POV survival hands](../../assets/series-lead/hands/pov-survival-sheet.png):

- **Zoom grammar (give every zoom a story job):**
  - *Stepped push-in cuts:* Step from wide → medium → tight ECU across 2–4 cuts as the narrator names escalating details, closing physical distance on each noun.
  - *Slow creeping push-in (`5–15%`):* Keep tension holds alive by creeping slowly toward the lead's eyes or the threat instead of locking on a dead still frame.
  - *Off-center ECU punch-in:* Punch tightly onto one eye, sweat drop, scar, or prop detail off-center so it reads unmistakably on a phone screen.
  - *Relief pull-back → dread pan:* Loosen framing for a brief false-safety exhale, then pan smoothly off-center into empty darkness to reveal the real danger just outside the safe zone.
- **Looking-around POV & subjective camera:**
  - *Audio cue → eye-dart → whip-pan:* Show a quick sideways eye-dart and sweat drop on a sound cue, cut to a fast horizontal POV pan with subtle handheld tilt, and hold 1.5–2.5 seconds on the empty space so the viewer searches the frame with the lead.
  - *Downward prop POV:* Anchor the lead's cream-cuffed right hand in the lower-right foreground (`pov-pipe-grip`, `pov-flashlight`, `pov-door-lock`, `pov-shield-face`) so the audience immediately reads first-person ownership before returning to a third-person reaction.
  - *Over-the-shoulder (OTS) corridor depth:* Hold the lead's back of head and shoulder in lower-right foreground while the threat closes distance down a lighted axis.
- **Motions that intensify cutout actions:**
  - *Micro-acting inside holds:* Layer sequential cues — eye-dart, sweat drop pop-in, `12°` head tilt, breath rise, metallic glint sweep — so held beats feel cinematic without over-animating.
  - *Foreground parallax wipes:* Pass dark poles, doorframes, or pillars *in front of* the lead faster than the background during walks and sprints to amplify speed.
  - *Rhythmic POV beat cuts:* Compress urgent travel or lockdown sequences into crisp ~1-second tactile POV inserts (`door lock → light switch → brace/shield`) locked to hard foley snaps.
  - *Full-extension arcs on forceful strikes:* Straighten the striking arm cleanly through the impact arc (e.g., 1-handed melee `slash`) before snapping back into guard, paired with a brief impact shake or punch-in.

## Pace the story

For a **45–90-second short**, keep the same camera logic in a compact sequence. Let the first image establish the premise, show the event early, and use the clearest consequences to reach the payoff. In a roughly one-minute cut, spend the first few seconds on the hook and visible event, the middle on cause and effect, and the last few seconds on the callback or answer. Give a discovery one first-person view and its impact a third-person reaction when both help the story. Use a close insert when it advances the same action. Leave a brief hold for the strongest image or cue.

For a **3–5-minute episode**, plan roughly **6–8 story beats** as a starting point. Give each beat a visible change that creates the next question, decision, or consequence. Place the premise on screen early. Then progress from noticing the anomaly to testing it, paying a cost, choosing an action, and discovering what the outcome means. A repeated setup earns its next cut when the prop, position, condition, or interpretation changes.

- Use **2–5 seconds** as an initial timing range for many action shots. Let a short insert land quickly when it answers a specific question. Let an important reaction or empty sightline hold when the viewer has something to search for or a sound to wait for.
- Plan several camera setups for each major location. A long story can reuse a small set of locations, with wide, side, rear, and close compositions plus a handful of independent prop layers. Build separate spaces when the story moves or needs a clear visual change.
- Give the first half a reversal. It should change what the lead is trying to do, so the next stretch has a new purpose. Give the last section a decision and consequence, then a payoff that answers or changes the meaning of the opening.
- Introduce clocks, distances, counts, or state labels at the point they help the viewer judge change. Link them to shot events and caption emphasis. Reserve a short pause after a reveal or decisive line so the image can register.
- In the rough cut, flag shots where the camera changes but the viewer learns the same thing again. Trim the repetition, choose a better reaction, or replace it with a new action. Keep longer holds that carry a clear question and a readable change.

### A four-minute planning example

This is an original example showing how to distribute beats. The premise is a fictional apartment where each exit returns the lead to the same landing.

| Time | Visible progression | Suggested viewpoint changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00–0:12 | The lead opens the apartment door and steps onto the landing. A second door opens back on the same welcome mat. | Third-person setup → first-person reveal → quick reaction. |
| 0:12–0:40 | The lead tests where they are. They crease a paper marker and leave it under the mat. | Hand-level detail → side view of placing it → overhead reset. |
| 0:40–1:15 | A different exit leads to the same mat. The marker now has a second crease. | First-person search → matching close-up of the marker → third-person reaction. |
| 1:15–1:55 | After several attempts, the sequence reverses. The lead hears a door close behind them before they touch the handle. | Third-person wide for where the lead is standing → sound-led turn → hold on the suspect door. |
| 1:55–2:40 | The lead changes the test and wedges the door open. The visible landing empties while the lead stays at the threshold. | Foot-and-door contact → wider geography → face reaction. |
| 2:40–3:25 | The lead follows the one exit that changes the view and appears to reach a normal staircase. | Wide body action → point-of-view reveal → wider check that the route is different. |
| 3:25–4:00 | At the bottom, the original mat is waiting. The marker has been creased again. | Calm third-person descent → matching marker close-up → final silent reaction. |

The mat, door handles, stairs, and paper marker can be built as independent layers. Important motion previews would cover the first return, the marker placement, the sound-led turn, the door contact, and the final discovery. Each beat supplies the next question for the narration.

## Direct the voice and sound together

Use the approved series narrator and the existing local Chatterbox workflow. For a short, time the hook, a change in pace after the event, and the last line. For a four-minute first draft, start around **450–550 words** and adjust once the narration and pauses are timed. Use second person for immersive actions when it fits the premise. Build the read around a few changes in purpose.

| Delivery cue | What to write in the plan | What to hear or see in the edit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Opening** | Short direct hook, stressed premise word, first visible action. | The viewer knows where to look before the line ends. |
| **Observation** | Plain language and one concrete detail at a time. | The named object comes into frame on or near its spoken cue. |
| **Suspense** | Shorter phrases, an exact pause where a cue leads, then the next line. | Space for a sound, movement, or reaction while the narration rests. |
| **Explanation** | One clear cause or rule explained across one or two sentences. | A diagram, insert, or change in the scene makes the cause readable. |
| **Decision** | Less connective wording, stronger action verbs, and a conscious pace change. | Separate short shots show the choice and consequence. |
| **Ending** | A brief release, the sound or image that returns, and a pause after the last line. | The cue gets noticed before the source is revealed. The final image has a moment to land. |

Keep tone, stress, and hold notes beside the script. For example, write *“The handle moves by itself.”* Mark **moves** for stress, then leave about half a second of picture and room tone before the cut. Generate the complete sentence as speech and place the pause on the edit timeline. Audition two opening reads and keep the approved take. For later chapters, render complete sentences or thought groups, record the chosen file and gap, and align captions to the final spoken timing.

Write a simple sound cue list with the on-screen trigger, frame, and intended role. Bring a recurring sound in clearly the first time. Later, start it before the reveal to motivate a turn, or carry it briefly across the cut to connect two views. Duck ambience enough for speech and use headroom for important effects. Review the mix alongside the picture, especially the speech pauses and the last cue.

## Production and review

1. **Outline, map, and storyboard.** Identify the first event, each new question, and the payoff. Use an overhead map and annotate the first-person camera, third-person reset, eye direction, and visible anchors at each switch.
2. **Time the voice.** For a short, verify the timed shot list and the opening preview. For a long episode, build a full-length rough cut with key poses, simple movement, and scratch or selected narration. Watch from the opening through the payoff and time the chapters before rendering detailed animation.
3. **Preview the difficult ranges.** Make key-pose stills and short, silent clips for the major body actions, prop contacts, viewpoint handoffs, and payoff. Use 2–6 seconds where each action fits. Budget by the number of difficult beats and combine adjacent actions in a labeled local reel where useful. A range with a deliberate hold can be longer.
4. **Build with the existing local tools.** Pose the shared lead in OpenToonz and use original environments and props in the browser composition. Break complex motion into short ranges for review. Put the planned duration, shot boundaries, local audio, and timed caption cues into the episode's Remotion manifest. Keep the $0 local workflow and use FFmpeg for the mix, technical checks, and any documented fallback.
5. **Check the final format.** Use the reference aspect ratio of 9:16 by default and record the intended aspect ratio in the plan. On mute at phone size, check that viewers can identify where the lead is, whose eyes they are looking through, which prop matters, and what changed. With sound, check that lines, effects, pauses, and captions line up. Review the whole cut again after a timing change at a chapter boundary or payoff.
