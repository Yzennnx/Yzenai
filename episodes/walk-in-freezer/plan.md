## Story and sources

1. **Instant skin-to-metal freezing (4–14 s):** Bare skin carries a microscopic moisture layer from perspiration. Aluminum at −20°C has high thermal conductivity (~200–235 W/m·K), drawing heat from the contact surface faster than local blood flow can replace it; contact skin temperature can drop to 0°C in seconds, freezing surface moisture into an ice bond whose grip can exceed epidermal strength if yanked away. On-screen change: bare palm locks to the frosted bar, pulls taut, and flips palm-up showing a raw red strip across the center of the palm; in the wide return, the lead cradles the injured hand against their chest.
   - *Source:* National Library of Medicine / PubMed research on finger contact cooling on subzero metals (e.g., Geng et al., *Annals of Occupational Hygiene* / cold-surface contact studies via NIH) and standard cold-injury guidance against yanking frozen skin from metal.
2. **Peripheral vasoconstriction & violent shivering (14–32 s):** As core temperature drops into mild hypothermia (roughly 35–32°C), the hypothalamus triggers peripheral vasoconstriction—narrowing blood vessels in fingers and toes to shunt warm blood to vital core organs—and initiates violent involuntary shivering to generate heat. Cutaway shows warm core glow and arterial branches narrowing in the hands as blue cold creeps inward; returning to the freezer, the lead carries the red palm, frost-dusted collar/hair, heavier breath plumes, rapid shoulder/arm shivering, and fumbling stiff grip on the latch.
   - *Source:* CDC Hypothermia guidance, Mayo Clinic Hypothermia overview, and StatPearls (*Hypothermia*, NCBI Bookshelf / NIH).
3. **Shivering stops & paradoxical warmth (32–50 s):** Dropping into moderate-to-severe hypothermia (below ~32°C), glycogen depletion and thermoregulatory decline cause shivering to slow or cease, accompanied by clumsiness/ataxia and confusion. Exhausted smooth muscle in constricted peripheral vessels can undergo vasomotor failure (sudden vasodilation), sending warm core blood rushing to cold skin receptors and creating a deceptive flash of heat that drives paradoxical undressing. On screen: shivering abruptly halts, knees buckle into a shelf brace, a brief cutaway shows the vessel walls snapping open, and back in the freezer a warm cheek flush appears as the lead dazedly reaches up to loosen their burgundy tie.
   - *Source:* StatPearls (*Hypothermia*, NCBI Bookshelf / NIH) and forensic/clinical hypothermia literature on vasomotor paralysis and paradoxical undressing.
4. **Frozen interior safety release & severe hypothermia (<28°C) cardiac arrest (50–64 s):** While OSHA (29 CFR § 1910.36(d)(1)) requires interior walk-in releases, facility safety guidelines warn that condensation/frost buildup can freeze an unchecked mechanical plunger rod solid inside its housing. Below ~28°C core temperature (severe hypothermia), victims lose consciousness and become highly susceptible to fatal cardiac arrest (ventricular fibrillation / asystole). On screen: the lead slams the ice-encrusted green plunger twice with the updated proportional pointing hand—chipping only a tiny frost flake as nothing moves—then collapses heavily onto the frosted steel floor (`60 MIN · 28°C` → `65 MIN · 27°C`), the ECG flatlines, breath mist ceases, and frost encrusts their motionless body.
   - *Source:* StatPearls (*Hypothermia*, NCBI Bookshelf / NIH), Mayo Clinic Hypothermia overview, and commercial walk-in freezer maintenance warnings (daily interior safety release plunger ice-seizure checks).

*Assumptions:* The episode compresses the timeline of mild-to-moderate hypothermia (which in a real −20°C freezer in light shirt-and-tie clothing develops over tens of minutes) into labeled beats so the viewer can follow the physiological sequence within a 64-second short.
